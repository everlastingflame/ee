[[Physics 2]]
Two types of charges, negative and positive. Most objects in our every day life have a neutral charge, which means that there are an equal amount of negatively and positively charged particles in them. An object with this behavior is balanced, or more oftenly referred to as electrically neutral.

**Excess charge** can happen when an object loses or gains too many positive or negative charges, causing an electrically imbalanced system. This behavior can happen when walking along a carpet and becoming negatively charged, this extra negative particle known as the excess charge. Discharge or charging can happen in dry conditions, since in a humid environment the electric charge will be neutralized before you can acquire it.  

**Glass rod experiment**
When you rub a glass rod with silk, a portion of the negative charges leave the silk rod and move to the silk. To get this experiment to work, the glass rod has to be hung from a thread to be electrically isolated from anything else so that no surroundings can neutralize it. We get a second glass rod and rub it with the same silk cloth, collecting more negative charges from the glass rod, and when we push them together, they repel each other since they both have an excess of positive charges. When we get a plastic rod and rub it with fur, the plastic rod transfers some of its positive charges to the fur, creating a negative imbalance for the plastic rod. When we push the plastic and glass rods together, they attract. 

**Conductors**
A conductor is an object in which a charge can move freely, some examples include metal, tap water, and the human body. 

**Nonconductor**
A nonconductor (also known as an insulator) is an object that prevents charges from moving freely, examples include rubber, plastic, glass, and chemically pure water. 

**Semiconductor**
Material that are intermediate between conductors and insulators, examples include germanium in computer chips, silicon as well.

**Superconductor**
Materials that allow for charges to move without any hindrance at all, known as perfect conductors. 

**Conducting Path**
If you were to attempt to charge a copper rod with wool, you could ground the copper rod by holding on to a metal pipe, which is connected to the earth, and by doing so you would be unable to charge the copper rod at all. The reason is because you, the metal pipe, and the earth are all conductors, and the excesss charge from the wool will cause the rod and wool to repel each other, but keep the rod neutral since the rest of the charges can disperse throughout the other conductors. If you were to hold on to rubber, you would be able to charge the copper rod since there would be no other place for the charges to go. 

**Charged Particles**
The structure and electrical properties of atoms allow for these behaviors to happen. Atoms have electrically positive protons, electrically negative electrons, and electrically neutral neutrons, all packed tightly into the center of the atom, the nucleus. An electrically neutral atom has the same number of electrons and protons, electrons are attracted to the nucleus because protons are packed into the center with the neutrons. When atoms come together to form a solid, the outermost electrons become free to roam wherever they please, leaving behind positively charged atoms. The mobile electrons are known as conduction electrons. 

**Coulomb's law**
If two charged  particles are brought close to each other, they exert an electrostatic force on one another. The direction of the force vector depends on the signs of each charge. If the particles have the same sign, they repel each other, if they have the opposite sign, they attact each other.

$$
\vec{F} = k \frac{q_{1}q_{2}}{r^2}\hat{r}
$$
$k$ is the electrostatic constant, or coulomb constant, $r$ is the seperation between the two particles, and $\vec{r}$ is a unit vector representing the direction of the force. 

This equation is similar to newton's equation of graviational force:
$$
\vec{F} = G \frac{m_{1}m_{2}}{r^2}\vec{r}
$$
These laws differ since $G$ is a graviational constant, and graviational attraction is always attractive, but electrostatic forces can be either attractive or repulsive, dependent on the sign. TLDR: one type of mass, two types of charge. 

The SI unit for charge is the coulomb, derived from the unit ampere. The current $i$ is the rate $\frac{dq}{dt}$ at which a charge moves past a point in a region:
$$
i = \frac{dq}{dt}
$$
If we re-arrange the equation and represent each symbol with their units (coulombs $C$, amperes $A$, seconds $s$) we get:
$$
1 C = (1A)(1s)
$$
The magnitude of an electrostatic force can become:

$$
F = \frac{1}{4\pi \epsilon_{0}} \frac{|q_{1}||q_{2}|}{r^2}
$$
The constant $k$ has the following measure:
$$
k = \frac{1}{4\pi \epsilon_{0}} = 8.99 \times 10^9 \text{ N} \cdot \text{m}^2 / \text{ C}^2
$$
The quantity $\epsilon_{0}$ is known as the permittivity constant. It can appear as follows:
$$
8.85 \times 10^{-12} \text{ C}^2 /\text{N} \cdot \text{m}^2
$$
**Multiple forces**
If we have $n$ charged particles near a chosen particle known as particle 1, then the force on particle 1 is given as follows:

$$
\vec{F}_{1, \text{net}} = \vec{F}_{12} + \vec{F}_{13}+\dots+\vec{F}_{1n}
$$
$\vec{F}_{14}$ is stated to be the force on particle 1 given the presence of particle 4. The best way to solve problems of this nature is to find the force on one particle given all of the others, and draw a free body diagram. The tails must be anchored to each particle, then add all of the forces as a vector, not as scalars. The result is the resultant force, not the magnitude. 

**Shell Theories**
1.) A charged particle outside a shell with uniformly distributed charge on its surface is attracted or repelled as if the shell's charge were concentrated as a particle at its center

2.) A charged particle inside a shell has no net force acting on it given that the charge is uniformly distributed on its shell
